Somewhere around the third venue tour, most couples hit the same confusing
moment. One venue says "you'll want a planner." A friend says her designer
handled everything. A coordinator emails you a package that mentions "design
support." And you're left wondering whether you're supposed to hire one person,
two people, or some combination you haven't figured out yet.

The confusion is fair, because the wedding industry uses these titles loosely.
But the jobs are genuinely different, and knowing the difference will save you
money, stress, and at least one awkward conversation where a vendor explains
that no, that's not what they do.

Here's how it actually breaks down.

## What a wedding planner does

A planner's job is the logistics of your wedding: the budget, the contracts, the
timeline, the vendor team, the guest list machinery. Think of them as the
project manager.

A good full-service planner helps you find your venue, recommends and books your
photographer and caterer, reads the fine print in every contract, builds the
master timeline, and runs the day itself so that the band starts when the band
should start. Their world is spreadsheets, schedules, and a phone full of vendor
contacts. When the cake delivery is late, the planner is the one making calls.

What a planner typically does *not* do is design the room. They might help you
keep your choices consistent, but most planners are not pulling fabric swatches,
sketching a ceremony installation, or building anything with their hands.

## What a wedding designer does

A designer's job is everything your guests see and feel: the concept, the
palette, the florals, the drapery, the lighting, the furniture, the way the room
makes people stop in the doorway.

A [full-service design and production company](/journal/what-full-service-wedding-design-includes)
like ours starts with your vision — yes, send the Pinterest board — and turns it
into a complete concept: mood board, color and material selections, floral
direction, lighting plan, rentals down to the linens. Then comes the part most
couples never think about. We don't just choose those things. We build, deliver,
and install them. The floral chandelier over your dance floor got there because
a production crew rigged it that morning.

<PullQuote>Planners manage people. Designers transform spaces.</PullQuote>

Both matter, but they are different skills, different teams, and different days
of work.

## Where the two jobs overlap

Here's where the confusion comes from: the middle ground is real.

Many planners offer light design help, like keeping your palette consistent
across vendors. Many designers, ours included, handle coordination work that
traditionally belonged to planners, such as full event coordination and timeline
management. And on the wedding day itself, the two roles work side by side: the
planner runs the schedule while the design team runs the room, the flip from
ceremony to reception, and the breakdown at midnight.

<Verdict label="The Real Question">
  The titles blur. The work doesn't. Don't ask a vendor "are you a planner or a
  designer?" Ask "exactly which parts of my wedding are you responsible for?"
  The answer should be a list, not a vibe.
</Verdict>

## So who do you actually need?

It depends on which problem keeps you up at night.

If your stress is logistics — managing fourteen vendors while working
sixty-hour weeks — a planner is your first hire. If your stress is the look,
because you have a vision and no idea who physically creates it, you need a
designer. If both keep you up, you need both, or you need a company that
genuinely covers both sides.

A few honest scenarios:

## The question that sorts it out in one phone call

When you interview any vendor, ask this: *walk me through what your team is
physically doing the morning of my wedding.*

A planner will describe confirming arrivals, managing the timeline, and handling
your family. A designer will describe trucks, crews, installations going up, and
a room transforming hour by hour. If a vendor claims full service, the answer
should include both, and it should be specific.

Vague answers in the interview become gaps on the wedding day, and gaps on the
wedding day become your problem at the worst possible moment.

This is also the moment to ask who owns the rentals, who installs the lighting,
and who is on a ladder at 7 a.m. The right vendor will love that you asked.
